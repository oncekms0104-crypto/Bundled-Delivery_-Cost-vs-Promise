How much does bundling cut delivery cost — and who pays for it in waiting time?

Korean delivery apps now sell two products for the same meal: 한집배달, where one rider carries one order straight to you, and a cheaper bundled tier (배민 알뜰배달, 쿠팡이츠 세이브배달). We asked a narrow version of the question this raises: for the same 100 orders, how much operating cost does bundling save as we raise the maximum bundle size and the delivery promise — and what happens to delivery time?

Why we asked this

The two-tier product is real, and so is the trade-off customers are told about: cheaper, but slower.

What we test: bundling lowers the operating cost of serving the same orders, and the size of that saving depends on (a) how many orders a rider may carry and (b) how much later than a direct trip the customer is promised the food. We model the operation, not the price: the fee gap is set by promotions and strategy as well as by cost.

What we found

  1. Pairs do most of the work

    Allowing 2-order bundles cuts cost per order by 37–42% at every slack we tried, which is 68–84% of everything bundling achieves. Riders fall from 100 to about 50. The saving is almost entirely rider fixed cost: choosing cheaper vehicle types without bundling saves only 0–0.2% here.

  2. Bigger bundles pay only when the promise is loose

    Going from 3 to 4 orders adds 0.1% at 15 min of slack, 8.5% at the original 30 min and 14.2% at 45 min. With a tight promise there are almost no feasible 4-order routes. The real 알뜰배달 cap of 3 orders sits where the extra saving is small unless the promise is generous.

  3. The wait is set by the promise, not the bundle size

    At a fixed 30-min slack, growing bundles from 2 to 4 adds 1.6 min to the slowest 10% of orders. At a fixed cap of 4, stretching the promise from 15 to 45 min adds 21.5 min. Each extra minute of waiting buys less: about 110 won per order between 15 and 20 min of slack, 20–30 won beyond 35 min. A cost-minimising dispatcher uses whatever slack it is given.

  4. The direction holds; the size depends on density

    In four 100-order instances the pattern repeats, but dense areas (~7×9 km) save 44–60% while spread-out areas (~16×24 km) save 31–47%, and 3→4 adds at most 4.4% there. Across four rider cost structures the saving stays at 50–60%; it is smallest when walking, the slowest and smallest vehicle, is the cheapest to dispatch.

What we would recommend

  • Treat the delivery promise as the main design lever for a bundled tier. It decides both how much bundling saves and how long the slowest customers wait.
  • Allow pairs everywhere: they capture most of the saving at little extra wait. Allow 3–4 orders only where orders are dense and the promise is at least about 25–30 min beyond a direct trip.
  • In spread-out areas, cap bundles at 2–3: the fourth order adds under 5% saving.
  • The real fee gap (6,000 vs 1,820 won, about −70%) points the same way as our modelled cost gap (−53% at a cap of 3, 30-min slack) but is larger — consistent with reports that promotions subsidise the bundled tier.

What this does not show

  • The optimum is over the candidate bundles the course algorithm generates (up to 4 orders), not over every possible plan.
  • The model minimises cost only. It uses all the slack it is given and even sends slow walkers for a 0.2% saving; a real dispatcher also values speed.
  • Demand is frozen. A different promise or price would change how many orders arrive and therefore the bundling opportunity.
  • Four instances, two per area type. Area size and shop-to-customer distance grow together in them, so we cannot say which one drives the difference.
  • Model cost is rider fixed cost plus distance cost. Platform rider pay, commissions and promotions are not modelled, so the cost gap is not a price recommendation.
  • Loose promises are computationally expensive: candidates grow from 1,388 to 234,207 between 15 and 45 min. The course default of 100 s cut generation short at 40–45 min, so we raised the generation limit (up to 30 min per slack) and checked that all other results were unchanged.

Method. The course algorithm (myalgorithm.py) generates candidate bundles of 1–4 orders, checking capacity, pickups-before-deliveries and every deadline, and Gurobi then picks the cheapest set that serves each order exactly once within rider limits. We changed only inputs and the candidate filter, in a marked copy of the code: deadlines are recomputed as ready time + direct car time + slack (slack 30 min reproduces the original file exactly); the maximum bundle size is enforced by dropping larger candidates before Gurobi; the baseline sets bike and walk availability to zero with a cap of 1. Cost = rider fixed cost + distance ÷ 100 × variable cost. Times are in seconds in the data (speed in m/s) and shown in minutes. Every solution was re-checked with the course util.solution_check. Raw outputs: summary.csv, orders.csv, fixed_cost_summary.csv, robust_summary.csv.
