Bundling halves delivery cost. The promised delivery time decides how much, and how long customers wait.

Korean delivery apps sell two products for the same meal: 한집배달, one rider per order, and a cheaper bundled tier (배민 알뜰배달) capped at three orders per rider. For the same 100 orders, we varied the maximum bundle size and the delivery-time slack and measured operating cost, rider count, waiting time and computation time, then applied the results to a lunch-delivery decision.

Our question. For the same 100 orders, how does the operating-cost saving from bundling change with the maximum bundle size and the delivery-time slack? Slack is how much later than a direct car trip an order may arrive: deadline = ready time + direct car time + slack. A 30-minute slack reproduces the instance's original deadlines exactly.

What we found

  1. Bundling cuts operating cost by more than half

  2. Most of the saving comes from pairing two orders

    Allowing 2-order bundles alone cuts cost by 37–42% at every slack and halves the riders to about 50. That first step is 76–84% of the max-3 saving. Choosing transport modes without bundling saves only 0–0.2%.

  3. The fourth order only pays when the promise is generous

    Going from 3 to 4 orders adds 0.1% at 15 minutes of slack, 8.5% at 30 and 14.2% at 45. With a tight promise almost no 4-order route meets every deadline.

  4. The promise, not the bundle size, sets customer waiting time

    At a 30-minute slack, raising the cap from 2 to 4 adds 1.6 minutes for the slowest 10% of customers. At max 4, raising the slack from 15 to 45 minutes adds 21.5. No order is late, but the model uses almost all of the slack it is given: in bundled plans the closest call arrives 0–0.8 minutes before its deadline.

  5. Density sets the size of the effect; max 3 is also the computational limit

    Dense instances save about 10 points more than spread-out ones (53% vs 43% at max 3, 30 min). 1–3-order bundles solve within 32 seconds in every case. 4-order candidates explode (4 → 165,156) and exceed the 100-second limit from 35 minutes of slack.

What we would recommend

  • Manage the delivery promise as the main lever. It drives both cost and waiting time; the bundle cap matters less.
  • Allow 2-order bundles everywhere. They deliver most of the saving with little extra waiting.
  • Allow 3-order bundles where orders are dense and the promise is at least 25–30 minutes. This matches the current cap of 3.
  • Avoid 4-order bundles. The gain is small unless the promise is long, computation time explodes, and in spread-out areas the fourth order adds under 5%.
  • Expect the fee gap to be smaller than the cost gap. Real total fees differ by about 15–28%, against a 53% modeled cost gap. Operating-cost savings are not fully passed on in fees; this experiment cannot say why.

What this does not show

  • A cost-only model. Mode speed decides only whether a deadline can be met; arriving sooner is not rewarded. The model even uses slow walkers to save 0.2% (slowest-10% wait 38.7 → 50.5 min at max 1). Real dispatch weighs speed too.
  • Optimal within the candidates. Results are optimal among the bundles the course algorithm generates (up to 4 orders), not among all possible plans.
  • Frozen demand. A different promise or fee would change how many orders arrive; this is not modeled.
  • Little data. Four synthetic instances, two per area type. Area size and trip length grow together, so we cannot separate them, and no instance uses real coordinates of a specific district.
  • Model cost ≠ fee. Rider commissions, platform fees and promotions are outside the model.

Method. The course algorithm generates every feasible bundle of 1–4 orders (capacity, all-pickups-first route, every deadline) for each rider type, then Gurobi selects the cheapest exact cover of the 100 orders under rider availability (20 bikes, 30 walkers, 100 cars). Travel time = round(distance ÷ speed) + service time. Cost = fixed cost per rider + distance ÷ 100 × variable cost. We left the original code untouched and added logging and a bundle-size filter to a copy; candidates are generated once per slack with enough time to finish, then Gurobi is re-run for each cap. Every solution is checked with the course's solution_check. The all-car baseline sets bike and walk availability to zero with max 1.
